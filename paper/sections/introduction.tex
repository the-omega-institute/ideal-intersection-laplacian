\section{Introduction}

The ideal intersection graph associates a graph to the intersection
structure of the nonzero proper ideals of a ring~\cite{intersection}.
For $\mathbb Z_n$, its isomorphism type depends on the exponents in the
prime factorization of $n$. We ask which of these graphs have integer
Laplacian eigenvalues.

Graphs defined by ring multiplication provide related constructions:
Beck's coloring graph and the zero-divisor graph~\cite{beck,anderson},
and the annihilating-ideal graph~\cite{behboodi1,behboodi2,aalipour}.
Their multiplication-based adjacency differs from the nonzero-intersection
convention used here.

The Laplacian viewpoint originates in algebraic connectivity~\cite{fiedler}
and its spectral development~\cite{grone,merris}; general graph-spectral
background is given in~\cite{cvetkovic,brouwer}. Equitable partitions and
generalized joins~\cite{godsilroyle,cardoso} reduce the present graphs to
small invariant matrices.

Nikandish~\cite{nikandish} develops a generalized-join formula for the
Laplacian spectrum and a Schur--Weyl reduction for equal prime exponents.
His results include nonintegrality for $(pqr)^k$ for every $k\geq1$.
His Question~(Q3) asks for the full integrality characterization; the
present arguments complete its three-prime case: every positive exponent
triple is nonintegral (Theorem~\ref{thm:three-prime-classification}).
Theorem~\ref{thm:unit-any-prime-count} also settles every positive
exponent vector with a unit entry and at least three prime factors.
An explicit zero-sum function on the connected complement has Rayleigh
quotient below one; reflection and the universal-vertex shift give
a graph eigenvalue in $(V-1,V)$, without a finite base.
Theorem~\ref{thm:small-exponent-rayleigh} extends this obstruction
to a distinguished exponent $m\geq2$ under the exact support-weight
condition $A\geq(m^2-1)B-(m-1)$, including equality. A rational
trial function factors the energy-minus-norm sign; its boundary value
is not a full eigenvector, giving a strict bound there as well.
Corollary~\ref{cor:small-exponent-reciprocal} covers every vector whose
other exponents are at least $m^2(t-1)$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:four-prime-pair-two} settles the four-prime vectors
$(2,2,b,c)$ by an antisymmetric invariant subspace. Positive principal
minors place its smallest eigenvalue between one and three; five
symbolic endpoint polynomials leave only $\{b,c\}=\{5,8\}$, whose
residual cubic is irreducible modulo $17$. This includes the balanced
vector $(2,2,2,2)$ outside the preceding sufficient condition.
Theorem~\ref{thm:four-prime-pair-three} also settles every
$(3,3,b,c)$, using tensor normalization for a general repeated-pair
lower bound. A second positive tail, eight nonsquare discriminants
and two rational factorizations exclude the integer endpoint without
an exceptional pair.
The remaining higher-prime case has every exponent at least two and
fails the support-weight criterion for each distinguished coordinate;
for four primes it also has at most one exponent equal to two and
at most one equal to three.

Two further complete residue classes are excluded by
Proposition~\ref{prop:mod-five-splitting}: every permutation of $(1,1,2)$
or $(1,4,4)$ modulo five. An irreducible quadratic or cubic in the
reduced complement quintic supplies the obstruction. On the equality
curve, the cubic case has square discriminant modulo five, showing
why the root equation gives information beyond discriminant congruences.
Proposition~\ref{prop:equality-local-discriminant} resolves a different
degeneracy on that curve: the reduction $x(x+1)^2$ has zero discriminant,
but its normalized discriminant is $13$ times a square modulo every odd
prime dividing $b$ on the branch $ac\equiv-1$. This excludes integer
spectra when $13$ is a nonresidue, in particular whenever $7$ or $11$
divides $b$, and reduces the six compatible modulo-five pairs to four.
Proposition~\ref{prop:equality-local-splitting} completes the corresponding
local splitting test, except the negative branch at $13$. In particular,
the two remaining $b\equiv0\pmod5$ rows split modulo every power of
five, by simple-root Hensel lifting. The global largest-root obstruction
proved below excludes integral spectra even in those locally split rows.
At the exceptional prime $13$, Proposition~\ref{prop:equality-thirteen}
uses the next discriminant term to exclude the negative branch unless
$b\equiv104\pmod{169}$. Its further local analysis is independent of the completed global
three-prime nonintegrality theorem.

We prove a complete classification for squarefree composite integers
(Theorem~\ref{thm:squarefree}), an explicit quadratic obstruction for
$(1,1,k)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:pqrk}), and nonintegrality for every
repeated-exponent triple $(a,a,b)$, $a,b\geq1$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:repeated}). The last result combines an arithmetic
restriction on the antisymmetric block with a consecutive-integer sign
interval for the symmetric cubic. The boundary family
$b=(a-1)(2a-1)$ is proved in Theorem~\ref{thm:boundary}.
An exact divisor criterion isolates every possible square discriminant for
fixed $a$. A second divisor criterion fixes the smaller-factor index instead,
giving a finite reduction over all $a$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:index}).
The first three nonboundary factor levels have complete finite modular
certificates. Every later level lies strictly below the uniform sign
threshold, closing the whole repeated-exponent family.

For a unit exponent, the complement of the general six-support quotient
has a nonzero eigenvalue strictly between $0$ and $1$, except for three
small pairs already covered by $(1,1,k)$. This proves nonintegrality for
every $(1,b,c)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:unit}), including fully unequal triples.
Positive-coefficient expansions at a minimum exponent of two, together
with one exceptional rational sign interval, then settle every
$(2,b,c)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:minimum-two}). Thus every three-prime exponent
vector with minimum entry at most two is covered. The same endpoint
polynomial gives a uniform cutoff $c\geq4a^2-2a$ for ordered
$2\leq a\leq b\leq c$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:distinct-tail}).
For a minimum exponent of three, this leaves exactly $325$ pairs.
An exhaustive integer sign certificate, positive second-endpoint
expansions and one rational exception settle all of them
(Theorem~\ref{thm:minimum-three}). Thus every triple with minimum
entry at most three is nonintegral.

For nearby unequal exponents, low roots can occur in pairs without an
endpoint sign change. A symmetric Schur complement gives an inertia
criterion for two roots in a specified interval
(Proposition~\ref{prop:low-spectrum}). At the upper endpoint three,
four bounded-gap families satisfy the criterion for every $a\geq4$.
Positive first-endpoint factors and root-free second-endpoint factors
exclude both possible integers. This proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:bounded-gaps}, including the full family
$(a,a+1,a+2)$ and every triple whose maximum minus minimum is at most three.

The requested diagnostic for minimum entries four through seven exhausts
the finite region left by the uniform cutoff. All $27\,562$ quintics have
positive nonsquare discriminants, verified by independent integer
Sylvester determinants. Together with the written tail and repeated-exponent
proofs, this yields Corollary~\ref{cor:minimum-seven}: every triple with
minimum entry at most seven is nonintegral. The dependence on the complete
finite certificate is explicit.

The Schur complement also admits a uniform comparison that covers mixed
diagonal signs. For every triple with minimum exponent at least four,
the complement quotient has a positive eigenvalue below three
(Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-low-root}). If
$(a-2)(a+b+c-2)>2bc$, that eigenvalue lies in $(2,3)$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-interval}). This settles the growing
balanced region where the minimum exponent is at least three times
the exponent span plus eight (Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-span}).
The universal bound also reduces any possible integral triple in this
range to the endpoint-zero condition $h_C(1)h_C(2)=0$.

An integer-scaling argument then settles every triple with common
exponent divisor at least three (Theorem~\ref{thm:common-divisor}).
Finite-field factorization settles every all-odd triple and explicit
common-residue classes, including residues $1,3,4$ modulo five
(Theorem~\ref{thm:congruence}). These arithmetic arguments allow
unbounded exponent differences and do not require excluding all integer
quotient roots.
Scaling also settles every common $2$-adic valuation
(Corollary~\ref{cor:common-valuation}).
Theorem~\ref{thm:mixed-residue-classes} collects the mixed-parity exclusions
and necessary conditions: six classes modulo eight, the full $(3,3,2)$
and $(3,0,0)$ classes modulo four, and the sum, derivative and binary-cubic
restrictions for the $(1,3,2)$ role. Its low-root consequences confine
$(1,3,2)$ and $(3,3,0)$ to the endpoint-two surface inside a linear region.
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-reduction} combines that linear bound, the endpoint
divisor constraints, the complete middle-exponent-through-fifteen result
and two further modulo-three exclusions. One worked shifted-root example
is given in the main text; all case calculations and endpoint details are
grouped in Appendices~\ref{sec:two-adic-lifting} and~\ref{sec:endpoint-details}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-two-completion} then completes the endpoint-two
alternative. Positive identities give $h_C(4)>0>h_C(5)$ on this surface
at minimum at least forty. The geometric bounds
$b<2a-2$ and $c<9a/4-8$ leave exactly $8\,658$ fully distinct integer
triples at minima eight through thirty-nine; every endpoint value is
independently checked by integer Horner and Bareiss determinant evaluation.
Together with the earlier repeated and small-minimum results, this proves
nonintegrality for every all-even triple and the full $(1,3,2)$ and
$(3,3,0)$ classes modulo four
(Corollary~\ref{cor:endpoint-two-parity-completion}). The complete positive
identities and finite-base counts are in
Appendix~\ref{sec:endpoint-two-identities}. The final low-root alternative is endpoint one, completed below.

Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-one-spectrum} proves the global gap
$\lambda_3<a+b<a+b+c\leq\lambda_4$ for real $2\leq a\leq b\leq c$,
using principal interlacing and a determinant-sign count.
On endpoint one it also proves a sharp exponent-sum lower bound for $a\geq2$.
For $a\geq4$ it places the smallest residual root in $(a,\min\{c,a+b\})$, with the
other three above $s=a+b+c$. Equality in the global lower bound occurs
only at $(2,2,2)$. The resulting constant-term divisor window in
Corollary~\ref{cor:endpoint-one-divisor-window} focuses the remaining
integer-splitting question. Its proof uses a quadratic with three positive coefficients
and a determinant-sign count, with no finite base.
Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-one-equality-spectrum} further identifies
$b$ as the simple third quotient root on $c=b(b+2)-a$ for all real
$2\leq a\leq b$, reducing the equality
splitting problem to a cubic whose roots all exceed $s$. Corollary~\ref{cor:endpoint-one-divisor-window}
also sharpens the smallest-root window using the exact coefficients and
a convexity bound for the three larger roots' reciprocal sum.

The graph and lifting arguments are given explicitly, so the proofs do not
require a complete spectral decomposition. Appendix~\ref{sec:verification}
describes the exact computations supporting the displayed formulas and
finite certificates. Theorem~\ref{thm:largest-root-unit-interval} finally places the largest
quotient root in $(bc+s,bc+s+1)$ for real $a\geq2$ when $b-a\geq3$
and $c\geq a^2-a$.
On endpoint one, the exponent-sum bound supplies the latter inequality;
written unit brackets handle gaps one and two for every real $a\geq2$.
This proves the complete
three-prime nonintegrality theorem. The new real interval needs no finite
base; the combined classification retains the earlier $27\,562$- and
$8\,658$-triple certificates. The remaining higher-prime
classification concerns vectors with every exponent at least two.
