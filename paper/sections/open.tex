\section{Remaining questions}
\label{sec:open}

Theorem~\ref{thm:three-prime-classification} proves nonintegrality for
every positive three-prime exponent vector.
Theorem~\ref{thm:unit-any-prime-count} settles any higher-prime vector
with a unit entry. Thus the remaining part of Q3 has at least four
prime factors and every exponent at least two.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:small-exponent-rayleigh}, it also fails
$A\geq(m^2-1)B-(m-1)$ for every choice of distinguished coordinate
$m$, with $A,B$ computed from the other exponents.
Corollary~\ref{cor:small-exponent-reciprocal} excludes, in particular,
every fixed $m\geq2$ with all other exponents at least $m^2(t-1)$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:four-prime-pair-two} further excludes every four-prime
vector with at least two exponents equal to two. Thus a remaining
four-prime vector has at most one such entry.
The endpoint-one equations retain separate arithmetic questions about
exponent points, even though none of their integer quotient spectra split.

\subsection{Arithmetic of endpoint-one exponent points}

The spectral and coefficient reductions below remain useful descriptions
of the endpoint-one surface. The new largest-root argument settles
integral splitting uniformly; determining all integer exponent points
on that surface is a different question and is not required by the
classification.

\paragraph{Spectral and divisor windows.}
Write $h_C(x)=(x-1)F(x)$, $s=a+b+c$, $p=ab+ac+bc$ and $r=abc$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-one-spectrum} gives four real roots of $F$
strictly above $a$, and Corollary~\ref{cor:endpoint-one-divisor-window}
places their smallest in $(a,\min\{c,a+b\})$. Its integer value must
divide $N_4=rs(p+s)$, while all three larger roots exceed $s=a+b+c$,
without an equality or sign condition. The sign parameter
$E=c+a-b(b+2)$ refines only the smallest-root window:
\[
 E>0:\quad b<d<\min\{c,a+b\},\qquad
 E<0:\quad a<d<b.
\]
Since the three larger roots exceed $s>b$, the sign of
$F(b)=r^2E/(b-1)$ determines the side of $b$ on which the smallest
root lies. This gives the displayed refinements and, when $E=0$,
the simple smallest residual root $b$. The earlier
\href{https://github.com/the-omega-institute/ideal-intersection-laplacian/blob/4afe151e1dc7193e6776cc1b08d3606aa804238e/notes/endpoint-one-middle-root.md}{regime proof}
retains the original interlacing argument. Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-one-equality-spectrum}
also identifies $b$ off endpoint one on the equality surface.
The coefficient interval in Corollary~\ref{cor:endpoint-one-divisor-window}
further requires $N_4/D<d<N_4/(D-N_4R)$, where $D$ is minus the
linear coefficient of $F$, $U=\min\{c,a+b\}$ and
$R=2/s+1/(p+s-1-U)$. Both denominator and endpoints are positive.

\paragraph{Coefficient and exponent tests.}
Write $F(x)=x^4-\alpha x^3+\beta x^2-\delta x+N_4$. Every integer
root $d$ satisfies
\begin{align*}
 d&\mid N_4, & d^2&\mid N_4-\delta d,\\
 d^3&\mid N_4-\delta d+\beta d^2,
 & d^4&\mid N_4-\delta d+\beta d^2-\alpha d^3.
\end{align*}
For $e\in\{a,b,c\}$, the determinant identity
$F(e)=-r^2(e^2+3e-s)/(e-1)$ also gives $d-e\mid F(e)$ when $d\ne e$;
if $d=e$, the condition is $F(e)=0$. These are necessary tests, obtained
by reducing $F(d)=0$, rather than sufficient splitting criteria. The
\href{https://github.com/the-omega-institute/ideal-intersection-laplacian/blob/4afe151e1dc7193e6776cc1b08d3606aa804238e/notes/endpoint-one-root-hierarchy.md}{coefficient proof}
and
\href{https://github.com/the-omega-institute/ideal-intersection-laplacian/blob/4afe151e1dc7193e6776cc1b08d3606aa804238e/notes/endpoint-one-root-congruences.md}{exponent tests}
retain the exact formulas and diagnostics. Each candidate still requires
the actual root equation and the remaining factors.

\paragraph{An elementary equality problem.}
For $E=0$, put $c=b(b+2)-a$ and $q=ac$. Let $G(a,b)$ be the plane
septic in Remark~\ref{rem:endpoint-one-equality-curve}, and define
\begin{align*}
 A_3&=q+b^3+5b^2+8b-1,\\
 B_3&=qb(b^2+5b+5)+2b^5+12b^4+25b^3+16b^2-8b+1,\\
 N_3&=qb(b+3)\bigl[q+b(b^2+3b+3)\bigr],\\
 P_3(x)&=x^3-A_3x^2+B_3x-N_3,\\
 \Delta&=A_3^2B_3^2-4B_3^3-4A_3^3N_3-27N_3^2+18A_3B_3N_3.
\end{align*}
The generic identity is
$h_C(x)=(x-1)(x-b)P_3(x)+x(x-b)G(a,b)$, so this is precisely the
spectral cubic when $G(a,b)=0$. The equality splitting system would require integers $4\leq a<b$, $d>s=b(b+3)$ and $u\geq0$ such that
\[
 G(a,b)=0,\qquad P_3(d)=0,\qquad \Delta(a,b)=u^2.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:three-prime-classification} proves that this system
has no admissible solutions. The following algebraic equivalence still
explains the earlier diagnostic tests. The curve equation is essential
to the formulation. A square
discriminant alone does not imply integral splitting: $x^3-3x+1$ has
discriminant $81$ and no rational root. With an integer root $d$, however,
$P_3=(x-d)[x^2+(d-A_3)x+N_3/d]$. If $P_3'(d)\ne0$, then
\[
 \Delta=P_3'(d)^2\bigl[(A_3-d)^2-4N_3/d\bigr].
\]
A square $\Delta$ makes the integer quadratic discriminant a rational
square and hence an integer square; its parity gives integer roots.
If $P_3'(d)=0$, the repeated root is $d$ and the third root is
$A_3-2d$, also an integer. Thus the displayed system is equivalent
to integral splitting on the equality curve. At each known point,
only divisors $d\mid N_3$ above $s$ need testing. The
\href{https://github.com/the-omega-institute/ideal-intersection-laplacian/blob/4afe151e1dc7193e6776cc1b08d3606aa804238e/notes/endpoint-one-equality-cubic.md}{explicit cubic note}
and its certificate retain the full $61$-term discriminant.

Propositions~\ref{prop:mod-five-splitting}
and~\ref{prop:equality-local-discriminant} supply further necessary
restrictions. The finite-field table first leaves six compatible rows;
the normalized discriminant excludes $(1,0)$ and $(4,0)$, leaving
\[
 (a,b)\bmod5\in\{(2,0),(3,0),(0,2),(3,2)\}.
\]
In addition, $7\nmid b$ and $11\nmid b$. These four rows permit cubic
splitting over $\mathbb F_5$; they assert neither integer equality points
nor an integral spectrum. The
\href{https://github.com/the-omega-institute/ideal-intersection-laplacian/blob/7d917afffd7c2616bc052810718dcc720d916a8b/notes/endpoint-one-equality-mod-five.md}{complete residue table}
records all nine curve rows and the three excluded cubic reductions.
The two further exclusions use $G=0$ and the normalized discriminant,
rather than a nonsplitting reduction over $\mathbb F_5$.
At modulo three all six curve rows split, giving no further obstruction.
Proposition~\ref{prop:equality-local-splitting} proves that at every
actual equality point in the two $b\equiv0\pmod5$ rows, $P_3$ splits
modulo every power of five. The simple positive-branch roots lift by
Hensel's lemma, so increasing that modulus cannot exclude those rows.
For every fixed $b\in5\mathbb Z_5$, the curve itself has a unique point
in each of the residues $a\equiv2,3$, with split cubics. Thus neither
row can be excluded by a finite five-power curve-and-splitting test.
At odd prime divisors of $b$ on the negative branch, the residue test
for $13$ is also sufficient for local splitting, except at $p=13$.
At $p=13$, Proposition~\ref{prop:equality-thirteen} requires
$b\equiv104\pmod{169}$ on the negative branch for integer splitting;
every other $13$-divisibility class has odd discriminant valuation.
These local compatibility classes no longer leave an open global
integral-spectrum case: the largest-root interval excludes all of them
at admissible integer exponent points. Further local analysis remains
a separate arithmetic question.

\paragraph{Finiteness and effective classification.}
Remark~\ref{rem:endpoint-one-equality-curve} and Siegel's theorem show
that the equality curve has finitely many integer points. They provide
neither a height bound nor an exhaustive list here. Riemann--Roch spaces
for specified divisors do not by themselves enumerate those points.
Effective enumeration of equality exponent points remains open.
Their integral cubic spectra, and integral quartic spectra away from
equality, are now excluded by the global three-prime theorem.
The endpoint-one surface itself is not claimed to have no integer points.
The genus consequence remains a structural remark; no Jacobian rank
bound or covering computation is assumed in the spectral results.

\subsection{More prime factors}

Which nonsquarefree exponent vectors with more than three prime factors
give integral ideal intersection graphs? The present arguments do not
supply the full general-exponent Q3 characterization. The distinction
between the repeated-exponent blocks remains useful: the boundary family
has an integral antisymmetric block, whereas $(1,1,2)$ has an integral
symmetric cubic and a nonintegral full spectrum. A general reduction must
control all remaining blocks.
