\subsection{Mixed-parity residue classes}
\label{sec:mixed-parity-congruence}

Shifted reductions of the complement quintic organize the mixed-parity
restrictions in one statement. The exponent letters in the residue rows
identify roles, independently of their increasing order.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:mixed-residue-classes}
For positive three-prime exponents, the following conclusions hold for
every permutation of the indicated residue roles.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Each of the classes
\[
 (1,3,2),\ (1,7,2),\ (3,7,2),\ (3,5,6),\ (3,7,6),\ (5,7,6)\pmod8
\]
is nonintegral. The classes $(3,3,2)$ and $(3,0,0)$ modulo four are
also nonintegral. These exclusions have no size or ratio bound.
\item For roles $a\equiv1$, $b\equiv3$, $c\equiv2\pmod4$, an integral
graph requires
\[
 a+c\equiv b(b+2)\pmod{128},\qquad b\equiv11\text{ or }15\pmod{16}.
\]
With $u=(a-1)/4$ and $t=(a+c-b(b+2))/128$, it further requires
\[
 \begin{cases}
 t+u+v\equiv1\pmod2,&b=16v+11,\\
 t+v\equiv1\pmod2,&b=16v+15.
 \end{cases}
\]
\item For either $(1,3,2)$ or $(3,3,0)$ modulo four, if
$m=\min\{a,b,c\}\geq4$ and $M=\max\{a,b,c\}$, an integral graph requires
\[
 h_C(2)=0,\qquad M<7m-16+\frac{40}{m+2}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
Every violation of a necessary condition proves nonintegrality.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The case table and complete shifted-polynomial arguments are in
Appendix~\ref{sec:two-adic-lifting}. The common mechanism is that an
integer-root factorization of $h_C$ forces its shifted binary cubics to
split into linear factors; their residue and derivative conditions give
the first two parts. For the last part, the odd roots would be three
modulo four, excluding one. The positive root below three from
Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-low-root} must then equal two, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-reduction} gives the maximum bound.
\end{proof}

For one worked example, take the residue roles $(a,b,c)=(19,27,10)$,
so $(a,b,c)=(4u+3,4v+3,4w+2)$ with $(u,v,w)=(4,6,2)$.
The shifted quintic satisfies
\[
 h_C(2y+1)/8\in\mathbb Z[y],\qquad h_C(2y+1)/8\equiv y^3\pmod2.
\]
Integer roots would therefore force all three odd roots to be one modulo
four and $16\mid h_C'(1)$. But
$h_C'(1)\equiv8(u+v+1)\equiv8\pmod{16}$, a contradiction.
The same identity proves the whole $(3,3,2)$ class in Appendix B.
The arithmetic conditions in this theorem do not assert integral examples.
Corollary~\ref{cor:endpoint-two-parity-completion} below completes the
remaining $(1,3,2)$ and $(3,3,0)$ classes modulo four.
